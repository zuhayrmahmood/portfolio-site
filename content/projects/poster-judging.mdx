My sister hosts conferences pretty often, however, there is one problem: the judging process. Currently, she hands out sheets of paper to the judges
for them to fill out and return. This gets tedious really fast, sometimes there are judges that add up their totals wrong, sometimes they don't return their sheet *(and she has to chase them down)*. 
All in all, it's a mess. 

A few days ago, she asked me if I could build her a platform to make the judging process easier, and I couldn't say no to the opportunity of learning something new.

## What it does

This platform allows judges to log in (via an access code the organizer generates and hands to them on a printed card, *no one wants to make a pesky account for a single event*). 
After logging in, judges can submit their scores for each poster they were assigned (the organizer can assign posters), they submit it and ta-dah! The posters are all rated, and
the organizer isn't chasing down judges for their scores, correcting their math or doing a [mad minute](https://www.superteacherworksheets.com/math-drills-minute.html)
to tally up the results.


## How it works

This is a Next.js 16 app that replaces paper judging sheets with a web platform. It has a few significant parts to it.

### Main Pages

There are two main pages in this app, the organizer page *(only organizers can see this page, and only for their own events)*, and
the judge page. Judges receive a unique access code from the organizer, which they can use to log in and
start judging presentations/posters. Each judge has specific posters assigned to them, and once their scores
are submitted, the organizer can view the results and see which posters received which scores from who. 

### Under the hood

Under the hood, this app uses Next.js and the backend is powered by a Postgres database (hosted on Supabase).
I've written a breakdown on some of the components of the app and how they work:

**Logging in:** Each judge gets an 8-character code like `K7M2-Q9XR`. The characters come from an alphabet
with all the lookalikes removed *(no `0`/`O`, no `1`/`I`/`L`)*, so nobody squinting at a card in a loud hall
types the wrong thing *(and then starts getting mad at the organizer)*. The database never stores the code itself, only a hash of it, so the organizer sees
each code exactly once, when they're generated. When a judge types their code in, it gets hashed, finds
the matching judge, and gives their phone a signed cookie that lasts the event day. To stop people from guessing
codes, a code gets locked out after 10 wrong tries in 15 minutes. That limit is tied to the *code*. Organizers log in separately, with a normal email
and password through Supabase Auth, and for them, they'll get an account that they can use.

**Scoring.** The organizer sets up the rubric, and each criterion has its own maximum and its own weight.
To get a total, each score becomes a fraction of its maximum, gets multiplied by its weight, and the whole
thing is averaged into a percentage. That means a 1–5 criterion and a 1–10 criterion can live on the same sheet.
For example, *Clarity* 4/5 (weight 1) and *Research* 7/10 (weight 2) works out to
(0.8 × 1 + 0.7 × 2) / 3 ≈ **73%**. This is set to change depending on user feedback and organizer needs. 

**Making the rankings fair.** Averaging every judge's totals for a poster gives you its **raw** score, which is
the number you'd have gotten on paper. The problem is that some judges are harsh and some are generous, and
no two posters get seen by the same group of judges, so a poster that happens to draw three tough judges gets
punished for it. That's why the dashboard also has a **normalized** ranking: for every score, it asks "how far
above or below *this judge's own average* is this?", and then averages those. A 70% from a judge who usually gives
60% counts for more than a 70% from one who usually gives 85%. Two edge cases are handled on purpose: a judge who
has only scored one poster (or gave everything the same score) counts as neutral, and a poster nobody has judged
yet is *unranked*, not zero.

All of this math lives in the database as SQL views, so the dashboard just asks Postgres for the results and
refreshes every 15 seconds. There's a copy of the same math in TypeScript *(so judges can see their total before
they submit)*, and the tests check that both versions give the same answers so they can't quietly drift apart.

**Assigning posters.** Posters get lined up in the order you'd walk past them (aisle, then poster number).
That line gets cut into one equal chunk per judge, and each poster goes to the judges who own its chunk and the
next couple of chunks after it *(three judges per poster by default, the organizer can change that)*. This way,
every judge gets basically the same number of posters, *and* their posters form one continuous stretch of the hall,
so nobody is zigzagging between opposite corners. The list is just a suggested route, though. If a judge wanders
over to the poster next door, they can still score it.

**Surviving bad wifi.** Poster halls have terrible wifi, so the scoring form assumes the connection will drop.
Every tap saves the sheet on the phone itself, so a dead battery or an accidental back-swipe doesn't lose
anything. If a submit fails, it goes into an outbox and the judge just moves on to the next poster. A banner shows
what's still pending, and the app retries every 20 seconds and as soon as the phone is back online. Sending the
same sheet twice is harmless, because the database keeps exactly one sheet per judge per poster and a resend
just overwrites it instead of counting it twice.

**Keeping it locked down.** The browser never talks to the database directly. Every table in Supabase is locked so
the public key can't read or write anything, and all reads and writes go through the server, which checks who you
are first. Events belong to organizations, so two people running two different conferences on the same deployment
can't see each other's posters, judges or results.
